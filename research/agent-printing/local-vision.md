# Local bed-clearance and print-failure vision research

**Question:** What local approaches can compare fresh printer-camera frames against per-printer empty-bed references and monitor active prints for failures? This is a capability and validation review, not evidence that any of the six named printers exposes a usable camera feed or remote-control interface.

## Finding

Use local, deterministic image checks as the first gate for **bed clearance**, with an explicit unknown state. Treat that as one input to readiness, never as proof that a printer is safe or correctly prepared. For **active-print monitoring**, evaluate an existing self-hostable detector such as Obico and/or a print-aware layer comparison. The user has selected automatic pause after failure detection is validated, and requires asking before resuming; no automatic cancellation is authorized. Until the detector passes printer-specific validation, alarms should request human review. The user has also directed the system to hold and ask when the camera is unavailable, stale, or uncertain before a start; this requires fail-closed semantics and validation on every camera/printer configuration.

## Bed-clearance: what vision can establish

The fixed camera view can compare a current image to a trusted, per-printer empty-bed reference and estimate whether visible regions of the plate changed. A simple pipeline is: obtain a fresh frame; verify frame identity/freshness and image quality; select a calibrated plate region; align it to the reference using stable features; normalize limited exposure variation; compute a difference mask and connected changed regions; then return `clear`, `occupied`, or `unknown` with the images and reason. Keep a still image and stable plate landmarks as part of the reference. Save reference provenance (printer/camera identity, capture time, firmware/camera configuration if known, plate type, camera pose, and operator confirmation) and mark it invalid when any of those conditions change.

OpenCV provides background subtraction and foreground-mask methods, and intensity-based `findTransformECC` alignment. Those are useful building blocks, not a bed detector: background subtraction models changing pixels and is designed for static-camera scenes; ECC estimates an image warp and can fail to converge, while large displacements need a good initial alignment. [OpenCV background subtraction](https://docs.opencv.org/4.13.0/d1/dc5/tutorial_background_subtraction.html), [OpenCV image alignment API](https://docs.opencv.org/4.13.0/dc/d6b/group__video__track.html)

The empty-bed reference must be established through a trusted enrollment event. A captured image alone cannot establish that the bed is actually empty: the onboarding flow should present the live frame for the owner to confirm, bind the reference to the specific printer and its camera, and capture several frames under normal lighting. A reference captured while an object is present would teach a difference-based checker that the object is the normal background. Do not silently update the baseline from routine frames; otherwise a slowly introduced or persistent object can become the new “empty” state. Recapture after camera movement, a plate swap, major lighting changes, camera replacement/firmware change, or a mismatch that cannot be explained. This recommendation follows from the static-scene/background initialization model and from print-imaging research that required preliminary modeling to match orientation, color rendering, lighting, and other working-environment parameters. [OpenCV background model initialization/update](https://docs.opencv.org/4.13.0/d1/dc5/tutorial_background_subtraction.html), [Petsiuk & Pearce, layer-wise anomaly detection](https://arxiv.org/abs/2111.02703)

The plate ROI and transform must be specific to each camera/plate configuration. The camera may see a moving bed, toolhead, gantry, cable, reflections, clips, or shadows; compare only the plate region after alignment and either mask known moving hardware or require the bed/toolhead to be in a known parked position. A change in plate type, plate position, camera angle, zoom, focus, exposure, or illumination should produce `unknown` until reviewed or recalibrated. Do not learn away scene changes by repeatedly updating the reference.

Image differencing will be weakest for small items, objects with similar appearance to the plate, thin wires, transparent/translucent parts, low-contrast residues, and anything hidden by clips or the printer geometry. A top or oblique view cannot see all surfaces. A second local object-presence model may help flag objects in a defined bed polygon, but a general pretrained detector is not automatically trained to recognize every possible user object or transparent scrap. A clear result therefore means “no detectable change in the observed region under this validated configuration,” not “bed is physically empty.”

## Freshness and camera health

Require more than a successful HTTP response or an open stream. The acquisition layer should attach a monotonic receive time and, where the camera protocol exposes one, source capture time/frame sequence. Require evidence of recent source captures or advancing frame identifiers; identical pixel content in a genuinely static scene is not by itself proof of a freeze. Reject known stale captures, decode errors, implausible resolution changes, excessive blur/darkness/occlusion, or a capture timestamp older than a configured limit. Re-check immediately before job start rather than reusing a prior readiness decision. If the camera cannot supply source timestamps, repeated-frame detection can flag possible freezes but cannot establish liveness; report that reduced evidence and hold for owner confirmation when freshness is uncertain, as requested.

The freshness threshold and distinct-frame rule are operational parameters to be set from measured camera cadence and latency, not guessed in the specification. Test frozen streams that still return a valid last image, stale timestamps, reconnects, and two printers accidentally swapped to each other’s feeds. Store camera-health state separately from vision classification so a valid “occupied” result is distinguishable from “no current observation.”

## Print-failure monitoring approaches

**Existing self-hosted detector.** Obico (formerly The Spaghetti Detective) is an open-source, self-hostable print-monitoring system with an ML API service in its official Docker Compose deployment. Its docs describe AI analysis of webcam images during prints and document false alarms from bed clips, vases, reflections, and logos. This makes it a practical candidate for a local evaluation, not a validated fit for this fleet. The official Compose file includes separate `ml_api`, `web`, `tasks`, and Redis services; the official published ML API image lists Linux `amd64` and `arm64` variants. Confirm current install/version/runtime and that all six printer feeds can be reached without a cloud route before selection. [Obico Server Compose](https://github.com/TheSpaghettiDetective/obico-server/blob/release/docker-compose.yml), [ML API image platforms](https://github.com/TheSpaghettiDetective/obico-server/pkgs/container/ml_api), [Obico false-alarm guidance](https://www.obico.io/docs/user-guides/failure-detection-false-alarms/)

The Obico docs themselves warn that failure detection may be off when print status is not recognized or detection is disabled, and that false alarms are expected. Its first-layer feature asks for a close-up nozzle camera to analyze under/over-extrusion and bed adhesion, implying a standard wide camera and a nozzle-view model have different evidence and coverage. Current docs focus on OctoPrint/Klipper integrations; compatibility with the owner’s stock Creality firmware/camera streams remains unverified. [Obico detector status conditions](https://www.obico.io/docs/user-guides/detective-not-watching/), [Obico nozzle-camera requirements and claimed scope](https://www.obico.io/docs/user-guides/first_layer_ai/nozzle-camera-configuration-octoprint/)

**Print-aware comparison.** A research implementation renders expected layer images from G-code and compares them to fixed-camera images using local HOG features and similarity measures. Its authors report that the environment needs preliminary modeling to match pose, color rendering, and lighting, and compare six representative error types. This is a useful research direction if the slicer can provide the exact G-code and layer timing and the camera view stays stable, but it is more tightly coupled to camera calibration and motion masking than simple presence detection. It does not establish coverage of all materials, geometries, printers, or defects. [Petsiuk & Pearce (2021)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2111.02703)

**Local model runtime.** Apple documents Core ML as on-device inference using CPU, GPU, or Neural Engine and no network connection requirement. That is a reasonable local runtime for a purpose-trained or converted classifier on a compatible Mac, subject to actual Mac hardware/OS and model conversion testing. Standard OpenCV is another local option for image processing. Neither runtime provides a suitable 3D-print failure model by itself. The report does not recommend sending frames to a remote vision model; a local-only deployment should verify network egress and any model/service configuration, particularly if adopting software with cloud options. [Apple Core ML overview](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/coreml/)

## What a camera can and cannot observe during printing

A wide fixed webcam can sometimes flag visible spaghetti, a part detached from the plate, gross layer displacement, an unexpected object or obstruction, or a stopped/abnormal visual scene. What it sees depends on camera angle, resolution, lighting, print height, toolhead/gantry occlusion, enclosure reflections, and object geometry. Obico documents user-reported false alarms from benign bed contents and reflections; its first-layer documentation calls for a dedicated close nozzle camera to inspect adhesion and extrusion details. [Obico false alarms](https://www.obico.io/docs/user-guides/failure-detection-false-alarms/), [Obico first-layer camera scope](https://www.obico.io/docs/user-guides/first_layer_ai/nozzle-camera-configuration-octoprint/)

One camera image cannot reliably establish hidden under-extrusion, internal voids, dimensional accuracy, material identity, a weak bond, correct CFS spool/color selection, nozzle temperature, or whether a completed-looking print is sound and usable. A printer reporting `complete` establishes controller job completion, not part quality or success. Combine vision with printer-reported state, progress, temperatures, material/spool state, and error codes where supported; keep “job completed” and “owner accepted print” separate. The existence of official detector-on/off and print-state gates in Obico reinforces that monitoring state itself must be surfaced explicitly. [Obico detector state conditions](https://www.obico.io/docs/user-guides/detective-not-watching/)

## Validation framework and acceptance evidence

Build an offline, labeled evaluation set independently for each printer and camera configuration. Include confirmed empty plates across supported plate finishes and normal lighting; benign static plate markings/clips; representative small, low-contrast, thin, transparent and reflective objects; plate changes; lighting/exposure changes; camera movement/focus changes; hand/tool occlusion; moving bed/toolhead; dirty lens; blur; dark/overexposed images; wrong camera association; frozen frames; stale/repeated frames; and camera disconnect/reconnect. Record the observed frame plus ground truth; split evaluation by printer and capture session so near-duplicate frames do not leak across tuning and evaluation. Track false-clear events separately from false-occupied/unknown, and publish counts and examples rather than one headline score.

For print monitoring, use supervised prints and safely staged, clearly labeled failure examples that represent the fleet’s actual materials and geometry: adhesion failure/lift, detached part, spaghetti, extrusion interruption, layer shift, nozzle blob, and harmless looks-alike patterns/reflections. Record whether the event is visible from each camera, first detectable layer/time, alert latency, false alarms per monitored print-hour, missed visible events, and detector availability. Treat unobservable/invisible failure modes as explicit non-coverage; do not infer them from a clean camera view.

Acceptance should require all of these before autonomous starts are enabled: owner-confirmed enrollment of each printer and feed; no cross-printer feed mix-ups; stale/unavailable/low-quality frames always yield hold/unknown; reviewed test set has no false-clear under the agreed hazardous-object set; any environmental/configuration change invalidates the calibration; and a fresh check is performed at the start boundary. The owner should choose the acceptable residual false-clear risk after seeing test examples. For monitoring, establish per-failure-type recall and false-alarm rates on held-out real sessions, prove that stale feed is reported as “monitoring unavailable,” and require an owner-visible image and reason for every alarm. Auto-pause may be enabled only after the agreed validation evidence exists for that exact printer/camera configuration; make the alert identify the evidence and timestamp, then hold for owner review before resuming. Keep auto-cancel out of scope unless separately authorized. No unsupported confidence percentage can substitute for this evidence.

## Open facts for the broader specification

- Camera presence, stream protocol, field of view, frame cadence, and printer-to-camera mapping are unverified for the one K1 Max, three K1 SEs, and two Creality Hi printers.
- Printer firmware, whether supported status/control interfaces are available, and whether a local Mac can access camera frames during printing are unverified.
- Mac model, CPU architecture, memory, macOS version, local runtime/container support, and acceptable image retention are unverified.
- Queue selection must query the target printer’s **current** camera and spool/CFS state; a cached historical “empty” result cannot follow the printer between jobs.
- Owner response preference is settled as automatic pause on validated failure detection and ask before resuming. Detector qualification, expected alert latency, degraded-monitoring behavior, retry policy and how to distinguish a printer-reported completion from a successful, owner-accepted part remain open.

These unknowns should be resolved by non-invasive capability discovery and then targeted owner-reviewed camera enrollment and validation. This research did not connect to printers, collect live images, install tools, or run a benchmark.
